Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) has been tested experimentally in many different collision systems and at many different energies. It is a field theory describing the interaction between quarks, the fermionic building blocks of matter, and gluons, the bosonic force carriers.
This section introduces the basic features of QCD that are relevant background for the investigations presented in this thesis.

Gell-Mann successfully described the observed spectrum of hadrons using different flavors of \textit{quarks} with different fractional charges~\cite{gellmann}.
This quark model was highly successful, but it predicted fermions that appeared to violate the spin-statistics theorem, such as the $\Delta^{++}$ baryon, which contains three $u$ quarks with parallel spins~\cite{psqft}.
This problem was resolved by introducing an additional quantum number called color charge, consisting of three color degrees of freedom, conventionally denoted $r$, $g$, and $b$.
QCD describes the observed hadron spectrum in terms of colorless bound states. Mesons are colorless because they consist of a color--anticolor pair, while baryons are colorless because they contain all three colors or all three anticolors.

\subsubsection{QCD Lagrangian}
QCD is a gauge theory based on a local $SU(3)$ color charge symmetry.
Just as the $U(1)$ symmetry of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) implies the existence of a gauge field, the photon, which couples to electrically charged fermions, the $SU(3)$ symmetry of QCD implies the existence of gauge fields, the gluons, which couple to colored fermions (quarks)~\cite{thomsonqft}.
The elements of $SU(3)$ do not necessarily commute with one another; therefore, the $SU(3)$ symmetry is non-Abelian.
The non-Abelian nature of QCD allows gluons to self-interact, unlike photons in QED.

The QCD Lagrangian is given in Equation~\ref{eq:qcd_lagrangian}~\cite{peskinqcd}.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:qcd_lagrangian}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QCD}} = -\frac{1}{4}F^{a\,\mu\nu}F^a_{\mu\nu}
    +\sum_f \overline{\Psi}_f\left(i \gamma^\mu D_\mu - m_f\right)\Psi_f,
\end{equation}

$D_\mu$ is the gauge-covariant derivative, defined by

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:qcd_covariant_derivative}
    D_\mu = \partial_\mu - ig_sT^aG^a_\mu.
\end{equation}

$\Psi_f$ is the quark field of flavor $f$, $m_f$ is its mass, $T^a$ is a generator of the $SU(3)$ color group, $G^a_\mu$ is the gluon field, and $g_s$ is the QCD coupling constant. The gluon field-strength tensor is

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:qcd_field_strength}
    F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu G^a_\nu - \partial_\nu G^a_\mu + g_sf^{abc}G^b_\mu G^c_\nu,
\end{equation}

where $f^{abc}$ are the structure constants of $SU(3)$. The first term in Equation~\ref{eq:qcd_lagrangian} describes the gluon kinetic term and gluon self-interactions, while the second term describes quark propagation, quark--gluon interactions, and the quark mass term. The index $f$ runs over the active quark flavors, and the index $a$ runs over the eight generators of $SU(3)$. The eight gluon fields transform according to the adjoint, or octet, representation of the color group.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\alpha_s$}{a_s}, asymptotic freedom, and perturbative QCD}

The QCD coupling constant $g_s$ from Equation~\ref{eq:qcd_lagrangian} can be used to define $\alpha_s = g_s^2 / 4\pi$, which is the QCD analogue of the fine-structure constant.
However, the effective coupling of QCD depends not only on quark--gluon and gluon--gluon interactions, but also on higher-order diagrams involving interaction loops, which must be accounted for through renormalization.
The renormalization scale, $\mu_R$, sets the scale at which divergences are subtracted.
As a result, $\alpha_s$ depends on the momentum-transfer scale of the QCD interaction, $Q$, according to Equation~\ref{eq:alpha_s}~\cite{psqft}. At one-loop order, the running coupling is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:alpha_s}
    \alpha_s\left(Q\right) = \frac{4\pi}{\beta_0\ln\left(Q^2/\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}^2\right)},
    \qquad
    \beta_0 = 11 - \frac{2}{3}n_f.
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.7\linewidth,
        alt={The strong coupling decreases as the momentum-transfer scale increases, illustrating asymptotic freedom.}
    ]{chapters/background/sections/quantum_chromodynamics/figures/asymfree.pdf}
    \caption{The running of the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ as a function of the momentum-transfer scale, illustrating asymptotic freedom. Taken from Reference~\cite{ptac097}}
    \label{fig:asymptotic_freedom}
\end{figure}

Here, $n_f$ is the number of quark flavors active at the scale $Q$, and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$ is the characteristic energy scale of QCD, approximately $200$~\MeV, depending on the reonomalization scale ($\mu_R$). For momentum transfers well above $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}$, $\alpha_s$ is small and the QCD coupling becomes weak.
This phenomenon is known as \textit{asymptotic freedom} and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:asymptotic_freedom}.
In the asymptotically free regime, QCD calculations become directly accessible through perturbation theory, where observables can be estimated as a series in $\alpha_s$.
Perturbative QCD (pQCD) allows for highly precise predictions of many observables measured at high-energy particle colliders.
Conversely, for collisions outside the perturbative regime, corresponding to low momentum transfer, calculating observables becomes much more difficult.
Sophisticated theoretical approaches such as lattice QCD can perform these calculations, but they are computationally expensive.

\subsubsection{Confinement and Hadronization}
The observed hadron spectrum consists of colorless (color singlet) QCD bound states.
In fact, no colored bound state has ever been observed, a principle known as \textit{confinement}.
The principle of confinement can be illustrated by trying to pull a quark--antiquark pair apart.
As the $q\overline{q}$ pair is separated, gluon field lines appear between the two particles, similar to the field lines between an electric dipole.
Self-interactions between the gluon field lines keep them close together, creating a tube of color field between the two quarks.
Figure~\ref{fig:qcd_confinement} illustrates this process by comparing the gluon self-interactions in a quark--antiquark system with the field lines of an electric dipole and the compressed field lines of a color dipole.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.9\linewidth,
        alt={Three-panel illustration of gluon self-interactions in a quark-antiquark system: gluon self-interactions are shown on the left, electric-dipole field lines are shown in the middle, and compressed color-dipole field lines are shown on the right.}
    ]{chapters/background/sections/quantum_chromodynamics/figures/confinement.pdf}
    \caption{(left) Illustration of gluon self-interactions in a $q\bar{q}$ system. (middle) Field lines for an electric dipole. (right) Compressed field lines for a color dipole. Taken from Reference~\cite{thomsonqft}.}
    \label{fig:qcd_confinement}
\end{figure}

As the separation increases, this tube becomes longer; therefore, the energy required to maintain this configuration is linearly proportional to the distance.
The energy density of this tube is experimentally found to be approximately $1$~\GeV/fm, which explains why it is energetically favorable for colored objects to form colorless states~\cite{thomsonqft}.

Now that the principle of confinement has been established, consider the situation in which each (anti-)quark in a $q\bar{q}$ pair is given a large momentum that pulls the pair apart, as in sufficiently hard $e^+e^- \rightarrow q\bar{q}$ scattering.
As described above, the $q\bar{q}$ pair forms a tube of gluon flux with highly concentrated energy.
At some point, the energy of the gluon flux becomes large enough to produce new $q\bar{q}$ pairs, which in turn break the original flux tube into two smaller flux tubes, or `strings.'
The original highly energetic quarks lose some energy in this breaking process because energy is required to produce the new strings.
This string lengthening and binary breaking process will repeat itself until all the $q\bar{q}$ pairs are formed into colorless hadrons.
The result is two streams of highly collimated hadrons, one in the direction of the original high-energy quark and the other in the direction of the high-energy antiquark.
The formation of these hadron streams, which is what experimentalists detect, is called \textit{hadronization} and is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:qcd_hadronization}~\cite{thomsonqft}.

\subsubsection{The Parton Model and Deep-Inelastic Scattering}

The parton model provides a description of the internal structure of hadrons by imagining the hadron as a box of free point-like quarks and gluons, collectively called partons~\cite{feynmanphotonhadron}.
Since the hadron has momentum $p$ the partons each have momentum $\xi_i p $ where $\sum_{\mathrm{partons}} \xi_i = 1$.
The partons themselves are imagined to be practically free of interactions, which requires that the hadron have a large momentum.
The fast-moving nature of the hadron ensures that, in the laboratory frame, the internal dynamics between partons occur over a relatively long timescale.
When an experiment collides with the hadron, the internal structure is effectively frozen over the timescale of the collision.

The parton model was introduced by Feynman~\cite{feynmanphotonhadron}, but did not find acceptance until later experimental developments.
High-energy lepton-nucleon scattering played a fundamental role in determining the internal configuration of nucleons.
Taking $e^{-}+p \rightarrow e^{-\prime} + X$ scattering as an example, it is illustrative to define some kinematics using the diagram shown in Figure~\ref{fig:deep_inelastic_scattering}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.5\linewidth,
        alt={Diagram of deep-inelastic lepton-nucleon scattering, showing an incoming lepton scattering from a parton inside the nucleon and producing a scattered lepton and hadronic final state.}
    ]{chapters/background/sections/quantum_chromodynamics/figures/dis.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic diagram of deep-inelastic $e^{-}+p$ scattering, where the electron has initial four-momentum $k$ and the proton has mass $M$ and four-momentum $P$. Taken from Reference~\cite{ptac097}.}
    \label{fig:deep_inelastic_scattering}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:dis_kin}
    Q^2 = -q^2, \qquad x = \frac{Q^2}{2P\cdot q}, \qquad y = \frac{q\cdot P}{k\cdot P}, \qquad W^2 = (P+q)^2
\end{equation}

Here, $q$ is the four-momentum transferred by the exchanged virtual photon, $Q^2$ is the squared momentum-transfer scale, $x$ is the Bjorken scaling variable, $y$ is the fractional energy loss of the electron, and $W^2$ is the invariant mass squared of the hadronic final state.
The Bjorken scaling variable, typically called Bjorken-$x$, has the physical interpretation of the partonic momentum fraction ($\xi_i$) carried by the struck parton in the proton.
The above process is called deep ($Q^2\gg M^2$) inelastic ($W^2\gg M^2$) scattering (DIS)~\cite{ptac097}.

Experimentally, one can measure the scattering angle ($\theta$) and the initial ($E_e$) and final ($E_e^{\prime}$) energies of the electron, which can be mapped onto the above kinematic variables using the following expressions.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dis_exp}
    Q^2 = 2E_{e}E_{e}^{\prime} (1+\cos{\theta}), \qquad x = \frac{Q^2}{2\mathbf{M}\left(E_{e}-E_{e}^{\prime}\right) }, \qquad y = 1 - \frac{E_{e}^{\prime}}{E_{e}}
\end{equation}
For unpolarized electromagnetic DIS in the one-photon-exchange approximation, the cross section can be written differentially in $x$ and $Q^2$ as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dis_x_q2_cross_section}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\sigma}{\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}Q^2}
    = \frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{Q^4}
    \left[ (1 - y - \frac{\mathbf{M}^2y^2}{Q^2}) F_2(x,Q^2) + y^2 F_1(x,Q^2) \right]
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is the electromagnetic fine-structure constant.
$F_1$ and $F_2$ are \textit{structure functions} that encode information about the internal charge configuration of the hadron.
Bjorken predicted that, in the high-energy limit, the structure functions only depend on Bjorken-$x$ and not on $Q^2$ (i.e. $F(x,Q^2)\rightarrow F(x)$)~\cite{Bjorken:1969}
This is consistent with the parton model: if the internal structure of the proton is described by point-like partons, scattering at different momentum scales does not resolve any additional structure.
Confirmation that DIS involves lepton scattering from quarks inside the hadron was provided by the observation of the Callan--Gross relation~\cite{CallanGross}, $F_2(x) = 2x F_1(x)$, at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC)~\cite{SLACFcn}.\footnote{Gluons cannot directly interact with the virtual photon in DIS.}
This relation is a prediction for electron scattering from spin-$1/2$ constituents, whereas $F_1(x) = 0$ would indicate scattering from spin-0 constituents.

\subsubsection{Factorization}

While the matrix element for a given QCD process may be able to be calculated to some order in $\alpha_s$ an observable will have to undergo long distances and will hadronize before it can be observed experimentally.
Additially, for collision systems with hadrons the internal partonic configuration of the reactents must be considered
Therefore, any measurment with hadrons in the inital- or final-state is sensative to low-energy (non-perterbative) QCD interactions.
The running coupling of $\alpha_s$ in QCD can be exploited in order to derive predictions for lepton-hadron and hadron-hadron large momentum transfer cross sections.
This is done by \textit{factorization} theorems, which systematically seperate (factorize) long-distance from short-distance behavior.

This is set by the factorization scale, $\mu_F$, which partitions the calculation into the perterbative hard-scattering ($Q>\mu_F$) and the non-perterbative long range interactions ($Q<\mu_F$).
Schematically, this separation is represented in Figure~\ref{fig:qcd_factorization}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.95\linewidth,
        alt={Diagram illustrating QCD factorization, with a short-distance hard-scattering process separated from long-distance nonperturbative interactions by the factorization scale.}
    ]{chapters/background/sections/quantum_chromodynamics/figures/factor.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic representation of QCD factorization.}
    \label{fig:qcd_factorization}
\end{figure}

For example, the factorized cross section for producing a dijet system in a proton--proton collision can be written schematically as

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pp_dijet_factorization}
    \mathrm{d}\sigma_{pp\rightarrow JJ}
    \approx \sum_{i,j,k,l}\int\mathrm{d}x_1\int\mathrm{d}x_2\int\mathrm{d}z_3\int\mathrm{d}z_4\,
    f_i\left(x_1,\mu_F^2\right)f_j\left(x_2,\mu_F^2\right)
    \otimes \mathrm{d}\hat{\sigma}_{ij\rightarrow kl}
    \otimes D_{k\rightarrow J}\left(z_3,\mu_F^2\right)D_{l\rightarrow J}\left(z_4,\mu_F^2\right).
\end{equation}

Here, $f_i$ and $f_j$ are the initial-state parton distribution functions, $\mathrm{d}\hat{\sigma}_{ij\rightarrow kl}$ is the perturbatively calculable hard-scattering cross section, and $D_{k\rightarrow J}$ and $D_{l\rightarrow J}$ describe the fragmentation of the outgoing partons into the observed jets. The momentum fractions $x_1$ and $x_2$ describe the initial-state partons, while $z_3$ and $z_4$ describe the momentum fractions carried by the observed jets.

\subsection{Parton Distribution Functions}
