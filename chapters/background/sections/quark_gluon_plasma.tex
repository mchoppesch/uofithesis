\subsection{The Quark-Gluon Plasma}
\label{sec:QGP}

The prior discussion of QCD involved hadrons in a vacuum at zero temperature; however, studying strong dynamics at nonzero (high) temperatures or baryon densities provides a novel environment for testing QCD.
This QCD environment is very rare in nature and is found in only two places~\cite{QGP:instantons}.
The first is in the interior of neutron stars, where the baryon density greatly exceeds normal nucleon densities.
The second is around $10^{-5}$ s after the Big Bang, when the temperature of the entire universe was very large, comparable to the nucleon rest mass.
Additionally, this high-temperature regime can be synthesized in a laboratory with collisions of relativistic heavy ions.

Models have long shown that nuclear matter is influenced by its thermodynamic environment.
Hagedorn's Bootstrap model~\cite{QGP:Hagedorn}, proposed before the acceptance of the parton model, posits that the density of available hadronic states increases exponentially with the temperature of a system.
The result is that, at a given temperature $T_H$, the partition function for the hadronic states diverges, and any additional energy input into the system will create additional hadrons but will not increase the temperature.
The Bootstrap model estimated this temperature to be roughly $T_H = 160$ \MeV.

With the discovery of asymptotic freedom, it was found that the hadronic couplings become weak at high temperatures, leading to a state of matter in which the degrees of freedom change from hadrons to quarks and gluons.
This transition is related to the restoration of chiral symmetry in QCD~\cite{QGP:Review}; in cold hadronic matter, the pions are the Goldstone bosons of a spontaneously broken chiral symmetry.
This state of nuclear matter, originally described in Reference~\cite{QGP:AsymFreeQuarks} as ``quark soup'', has become known as the Quark-Gluon Plasma (QGP).
This led to the reinterpretation of $T_H$ as a phase transition point, i.e., the critical temperature above which quarks are liberated from hadrons~\cite{QGP:TH}.

The phase diagram for QCD matter is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:qgp_phase_diagram}.
The baryon chemical potential is related to the net baryon density.
At low baryon density, the crossover between the hadronic phase and the QGP is a smooth transition.
The nature of this transition is related to the nonzero quark masses.
As the phase diagram moves towards higher $\mu$, lattice calculations indicate that the transition may become a first-order phase transition~\cite{QGP:Lattice2}.
At very high baryon density, exotic states such as a color superconductor are postulated to exist, but this portion of the phase diagram is less well studied~\cite{QGP:superconductor}.
Heavy-ion collisions at increasingly higher energies probe the low-$\mu$, high-$T$ region of the QGP diagram because the ratio of baryon number to entropy scales inversely with collision energy.

    \label{fig:heavy_ion_collision_stages}
\end{figure}

The QGP exists for only a short time during a heavy-ion collision. To study its properties, it is important to understand how the collision evolves into and out of the QGP state.
The different stages of an ion collision are described in Figure~\ref{fig:heavy_ion_collision_stages}.

\paragraph{Initial Conditions} The collision consists of two ions overlapping with some impact parameter $b$, which can be related to the centrality of the collision (as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:glauber}).
Centrality affects the transverse volume and shape of the later QGP medium: a central collision has a roughly circular overlap region, while a peripheral collision has a more lenticular overlap region.
The partonic content of the interacting nucleons is also modified by nPDFs, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:nPDF}.

\paragraph{Pre-equilibrium/parton scattering}
As the nuclei cross each other at a time of 0 fm/$c$, most nucleon--nucleon collisions are of the low-$Q^2$ variety.
Nucleon--nucleon collisions also happen at a much higher $Q^2$, but they are a small fraction of the total $N_{\mathrm{coll}}$.
At $t>0$ fm/$c$, the two nuclei begin to separate at the speed of light, carrying most of the baryon density away from the collision point.
As the two receding nucleons are pulled apart, the \textit{glasma} state forms. It can be described as a ``color capacitor plate'' in which longitudinal color field lines form between the two ions~\cite{QGP:Glasma}.
The Glasma is not yet an equilibrated QGP, but it is an early state dominated by strong longitudinal color fields that evolve and produce particles.

\paragraph{QGP formation} Over roughly the first $1$ fm/$c$ after the initial collision, the Glasma evolves into the hot, dense matter that forms the QGP.
The system hydrodynamizes, meaning that viscous hydrodynamics becomes a useful macroscopic description.
The QGP behaves like a nearly perfect fluid, with a small shear-viscosity-to-entropy-density ratio, $\eta/s$~\cite{QGP:Review3}.
As time moves forward, the QGP expands hydrodynamically according to the pressure gradients in the system.

\paragraph{Hadronization and freezeout}
The QGP cools as it expands, with the temperature eventually falling below the crossover from QGP to the hadronic phase of matter.
During this phase, the unbound, interacting quarks and gluons start to hadronize.
At a temperature called the \textit{chemical freezeout temperature}, the partons have mostly become confined into hadrons, and no further inelastic interactions occur between them.
At this stage, the number of specific hadron types in the final state is fixed, although they may still elastically interact with each other.
At a later stage, kinetic freezeout occurs at the \textit{kinetic freezeout temperature}.
At this point, the spacing between the final-state hadrons becomes so large that interactions are negligible.
At this stage, the momentum spectrum of the hadrons becomes fixed, and the final-state particles free-stream towards the detector, where they can be observed experimentally.

\subsubsection{Soft probes of the QGP}

Observables related to the low-momentum bulk particles produced in a heavy-ion collision can provide sensitive probes of the nature, size, and evolution of the QGP.
Specifically, the azimuthally differential yields of final-state particles, typically referred to as \textit{flow}, are related to the transverse extent of the medium.
These yields can be written as a Fourier expansion of the $\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}\phi}$ distribution:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:flow_fourier}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}\phi}
    \propto 1 + 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
    v_n\cos\left(n(\phi-\Psi_n)\right)
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of final-state hadrons, $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle, $\Psi_n$ is the event-plane angle, and $v_n$ are the $n$th-order flow coefficients.
The event-plane angle is an estimate of the direction of maximal particle emission for a specific harmonic $n$.
For $n=2$, the event-plane angle roughly tracks the azimuthal angle of the impact-parameter vector $\vec{b}$.
The sum in Equation~\ref{eq:flow_fourier} is over all particles in a given event, though typically only charged particles are available experimentally.
The flow coefficients observed experimentally are averages of the coefficients extracted event by event.

For heavy-ion events with non-negligible $b$, the resulting fireball is less circular (left side of Figure~\ref{fig:qgp_flow}) and more lenticular (right side of Figure~\ref{fig:qgp_flow}).
The pressure of the QGP fluid is at a maximum in the center of the medium and is zero in the vacuum outside it.
Therefore, pressure gradients will form across the medium, which will be counteracted by the shear viscosity of the medium.
This gradient is larger across the shorter axis of the medium than across the longer axis, inducing more rapid hydrodynamic expansion along the shorter axis.
After hadronization and freezeout, the result of this anisotropic expansion, called elliptic flow, is encoded in the $v_2$ flow coefficient of Equation~\ref{eq:flow_fourier}.
Measurements of elliptic flow provide important input into calculations of the shear viscosity of the medium~\cite{QGP:Review3}.
In fact, early RHIC data seem to favor a very low shear viscosity of the QGP fluid ($\eta / s \approx 0.1$)~\cite{QGP:RHICetas}, earning the QGP the name ``the most perfect fluid.''
Figure~\ref{fig:atlas_vn} displays a measurement of $v_2$ (among other coefficients) as a function of both charged-particle \PT\ and event centrality.
For very central events (small $b$), the medium formed is roughly circular, and therefore there is little azimuthal anisotropy in the pressure gradients, leading to smaller elliptic flow.
In more peripheral selections (higher $b$), the fireball takes on a more lenticular shape, which leads to a higher $v_2$.

The zeroth-order flow coefficient, called radial flow, is not included in the Fourier decomposition of Equation~\ref{eq:flow_fourier} because it represents the collective isotropic expansion and does not vary with $\phi$.
The bulk viscosity of the QGP counteracts this radial acceleration; therefore, measurements of $v_0$ can help constrain this quantity~\cite{QGP:Review3}.
The left side of Figure~\ref{fig:qgp_flow} shows the isotropic expansion of the (orange) medium leading to radial flow.
The ATLAS experiment recently measured radial flow directly~\cite{HION-2024-06} in collisions with very small $b$, i.e., very central collisions.
Anisotropy in the $\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}\phi}$ distribution can result from either the geometry or fluctuations in the initial state.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.75\linewidth,

    ]{chapters/background/sections/quark_gluon_plasma/figures/v_n_atlas.pdf}
    \caption{The $v_n$ obtained as a function of transverse momentum $p_T$ in 11 centrality intervals, from the most central at the top left panel to the most peripheral at the bottom right panel. Taken from Ref.~\cite{HION-2016-06}.}
    \label{fig:atlas_vn}
\end{figure}

Another source of transverse anisotropy in the QGP comes from fluctuations in the initial state.
If the optical Glauber approximation of smooth nuclear densities were correct, then all odd $v_n$ coefficients should vanish.
However, due to event-by-event fluctuations in the nucleon positions, these odd harmonics end up being non-zero.
The $v_3$ is termed triangular flow, and it reflects triangular structures inside the QGP fireball.
Figure~\ref{fig:atlas_vn} shows that, in central collisions, azimuthal particle production is dominated by fluctuations in the initial state because $v_{3,4}> v_2$ for many bins of \PT.
Experimentally, measuring flow signals can be complicated by the presence of high-$Q^2$ processes. For example, a dijet will produce some azimuthal anisotropy that does not come from the QGP.
Methods for subtracting such ``non-flow'' contributions are used to circumvent this problem.

Other bulk observables, such as the charged-particle pseudorapidity density, $\mathrm{d}N_{\mathrm{ch}}/\mathrm{d}\eta$, and transverse-momentum spectrum, $\mathrm{d}N_{\mathrm{ch}}/\mathrm{d}p_{\mathrm{T}}$, can also be used to infer properties of the medium.
The pseudorapidity density constrains the produced-particle multiplicity and, in turn, the medium's entropy production and initial density.
The low-$p_{\mathrm{T}}$ shape of the spectrum and the mean transverse momentum, $\langle p_{\mathrm{T}}\rangle$, are sensitive to the medium's expansion and radial flow.
These observables are interpreted together with models of the initial conditions and particle production.

\subsubsection{Jet quenching and hard probes of the QGP}

A signature of QGP formation is the interaction of the medium with hard (high-$Q^2$) partons, which modifies the momentum and direction of the experimentally observed jets.
\textit{Jet quenching} describes how jets interact with the QGP medium and lose energy through a redistribution of energy from inside a jet cone to outside it.
Jets are produced very early in a heavy-ion collision and, as such, ``see'' the full lifecycle of the QGP.
The pre-equilibrium phase has been shown to contribute to energy loss, but the dominant effect comes from the QGP medium~\cite{QGP:preQuench}.

Energetic partons lose energy to the medium via both elastic and inelastic collisions with the medium.
Elastic $2\to 2$ collisions with the medium are referred to as collisional energy loss.
Parton splittings and gluon radiation, both present in the vacuum, become more prevalent inside the QGP due to multiple scatterings with the medium constituents.
This mechanism is called radiative energy loss, and it also includes inelastic medium collisions~\cite{QGP:JetQuenching}.
The two types of quenching processes are pictured in Figure~\ref{fig:qgp_jet_energy_loss}.

    \caption{Schematic illustration of collisional (left) and radiative (right) energy loss of an energetic parton in the QGP. Taken from Ref.~\cite{QGP:JetQuenching}.}
    \label{fig:qgp_jet_energy_loss}
\end{figure}

The strength of jet quenching can be described by $\hat{q}$, known as the \textit{jet transport coefficient}.
\begin{equation}
    \hat{q} = \frac{d\langle \Delta p_{\mathrm{T}}^2 \rangle}{dL}
\end{equation}
The jet transport coefficient describes the average squared transverse momentum lost by the jet to the medium per unit length.
Reference~\cite{QGP:Qhat} shows that $\hat{q} / T^3$ can be related to $\eta / s$ in both a strongly and weakly coupled QGP and argues that $\hat{q} / T^3$ is a better measure of the in-medium QCD coupling strength.
The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:qgp_qhat_and_length_dependence} shows that $\hat{q} / T^3$ increases as $T$ decreases, according to a Bayesian analysis by JETSCAPE~\cite{QGP:JetscapeQhat}.

The right panel shows the dependence of average jet quenching effects on the path length traversed through the medium~\cite{QGP:Ldep}.
Collisional energy loss grows linearly with path length, while radiative effects grow quadratically.
Additionally, gluon jets are quenched more than quark jets are because they have a larger color charge and therefore interact more strongly with the medium.
Additionally, a simple path-length dependence of jet quenching is complicated by jet-by-jet fluctuations in energy loss~\cite{QGP:JetQuenchFluct}.

The jet-medium effects described above can be written as a factorized formula, with the ``hot'' jet-quenching effects expressed in Equation~\ref{eq:AA_pseudofactorization}.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:AA_pseudofactorization}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{d}\sigma_{AB\rightarrow h+X}
        &\approx \sum_{ijkk'l}\int\mathrm{d}x_i\mathrm{d}x_j\mathrm{d}z_k\,
        f_i^A\left(x_i,\mu_F^2\right)f_j^B\left(x_j,\mu_F^2\right) \\
        &\quad\otimes \mathrm{d}\hat{\sigma}_{ij\rightarrow kl}\left(x_i,x_j, \alpha_s \left(\mu_R\right),Q^2 \right) \\
        &\quad\otimes P_{k\rightarrow k'}\left(z_{k},\mu_F^2\right) \\
        &\quad\otimes D_{k'\rightarrow h}\left(z_{k'},\mu_F^2\right)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The interactions between the hard parton $k$ and the QGP are parameterized by $P_{k\rightarrow k'}\left(z_{k}\right)$.
Note also that the PDFs in Equation~\ref{eq:pp_factorization} have been replaced by nPDFs for the relevant nuclear species.
The ``so-called'' medium-modified FF is given by $\Sigma_{k'} P_{k\rightarrow k'}\left(z_{k}\right)\otimes D_{k'\rightarrow h}\left(z_{k'}\right)$.
Although this formula is widely used in phenomenological studies of the QGP, no formal proof of this type of factorization is currently available~\cite{QGP:JetQuenching}.

\paragraph{Jet \texorpdfstring{\Raa}{Raa}} Jet quenching is studied experimentally by measuring hadron or jet observables in \AxA\ collisions.
One such observable is the nuclear modification factor, \Raa, which describes how the production of hadrons or jets is modified relative to an expectation of \NCOLL\ scaling.
Note that calculating \Raa\ requires the reference \pxp\ data to be taken under similar conditions and at a similar $\sqrt{s}$ to the ion data.
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:RAA}
    R_{AA} =
    \frac{\displaystyle \frac{1}{A^2}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\sigma_{AA}^{h/j}}{\mathrm{d}p_{\mathrm{T}}\,\mathrm{d}y}}
    {\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\sigma_{pp}^{h/j}}
    {\mathrm{d}p_{\mathrm{T}}\,\mathrm{d}y}}
    \quad \quad
    R_{AA}|_{\mathrm{cent}} =
    \frac{\displaystyle
        \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{evt}}\langle T_{AA}\rangle}
        \frac{ \mathrm{d}^2N_{AA}^{h/j}}
    {\mathrm{d}p_{\mathrm{T}}\,\mathrm{d}y} \bigg |_{\mathrm{cent}}}
    {\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\sigma_{pp}^{h/j}}
    {\mathrm{d}p_{\mathrm{T}}\,\mathrm{d}y}}
\end{equation}
\Raa\ is typically reported as a function of \PT\ and/or rapidity.
By varying \PT, one is sensitive to the dependence of quenching on $Q^2$.
By varying rapidity, one is sensitive to the flavor of the initiating parton, since gluon jets lose more energy to the medium.
The left-hand side of Equation~\ref{eq:RAA} represents a centrality-integrated \Raa.
The prefactor of $A^2$ accounts for the number of nucleons in the colliding ion species.
Note that this formula for \Raa\ is not sensitive to any Glauber-model uncertainties.
The right-hand side of Equation~\ref{eq:RAA} shows a centrality-differential \Raa.
Studying jet-quenching observables differentially in centrality is important because the energy loss is expected to be proportional to the path length in the medium~\cite{QGP:JetQuenching}, and centrality provides a systematic way to vary the medium size.
Here, $N_{\mathrm{evt}}^C$ is the number of minimum-bias \AxA\ events in a given centrality interval, and $\langle T_{AA}\rangle_C$ is the average nuclear thickness function in that interval, obtained from a Glauber calculation~\cite{Glauber}.
The nuclear thickness function satisfies $\langle T_{AA}\rangle_{\mathrm{cent}}=\langle N_{\mathrm{coll}}\rangle_{\mathrm{cent}}/\sigma_{pp}^{\mathrm{inel}}$. Sometimes, the numerator on the right-hand side of Equation~\ref{eq:RAA} is called the parton-flux-normalized yield because the Glauber factors normalize the overlapping flux of the two ions to that of a \pxp\ collision.

\paragraph{Dijet balance} Dijet observables are directly sensitive to asymmetric quenching experienced by the two jets.
This asymmetry can result from different path lengths through the medium or from jet-by-jet fluctuations in energy loss.
The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dijet_asymmetric_path} illustrates how the location of the hard scattering can cause the two jets to travel different path lengths through the QGP.
An observable used to characterize the quenching of the two jets in a dijet system is the dijet momentum balance, shown in Equation~\ref{eq:xJ}.
\begin{equation}
    \XJ = \frac{\PTTWO}{\PTONE},
    \label{eq:xJ}
\end{equation}
Here, $\PTONE$ and $\PTTWO$ are the transverse momenta of the leading and subleading jets, respectively, which are required to be nearly back-to-back in azimuth.

The event displayed is characteristic of a highly asymmetric dijet event, with one clear large-jet signature at $(\eta, \phi ) = 0,\pi$ but no recoiling jet at the expected location, $(\eta, \phi ) = 0,0$.
This measurement revealed that the dijet balance was modified to favor more asymmetric momentum configurations in \PbxPb\ collisions than in \pxp\ collisions, with a stronger effect in more central collisions.
This discovery prompted theorists to incorporate the highly modified dijet balance into models~\cite{QGP:Dijet2010Theory1,QGP:Dijet2010Theory2}, as shown in the left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dijet_balance_measurements}.

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:dijet_balance_measurements} compares the measured dijet asymmetry in subsequent \XJ\ measurements at 5.02 and 2.76~\TeV\ and shows little dependence on \SNN.

All the \XJ\ distributions discussed so far have been ``area-normalized''; that is, they are divided by the total number of events so that the integral under each distribution is unity.
Interpretations of these plots must account for the fact that shifts in the \PbxPb\ distributions relative to \pxp\ represent relative enhancements or suppressions.
ATLAS addressed this lack of information about absolute dijet yields in Reference~\cite{HION-2018-28} by introducing absolutely normalized \XJ\ yields, which introduce a Glauber-model dependence through \TAA\footnote{Technically, any centrality-differential measurement has a Glauber-model dependence. However, for wide centrality bins, such as those typically used in jet measurements, this uncertainty is usually negligible and is therefore neglected.}.
The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:xj_absolute_yields} shows absolute \XJ\ yields for several centrality intervals.
A striking feature of this plot is that the suppression of balanced dijets relative to \pxp\ increases with centrality, with more central events showing greater suppression.
The picture at low \XJ\ is less clear and depends on the \PTONE\ selection considered.
To study the differences between \PbxPb\ and \pxp\ data directly, Reference~\cite{HION-2024-03} defines the \Jaa\ observable as the ratio of the centrality-differential, absolutely normalized \XJ\ distributions in \PbxPb\ to those in \pxp.
The \Jaa\ distributions for different centrality selections are shown in the middle panel of Figure~\ref{fig:xj_absolute_yields}.
The interpretation is similar to that of the previous plot, but the experimental precision is increased by canceling correlated sources of uncertainty between the \PbxPb\ and \pxp\ measurements.

\subsubsection{Searching for QGP in small systems}
\label{sec:qgp_small}

The umberella term of small systems covers many different collision scenarios all with different advantages and challenges.
Generally \pxp, \pxA, \AxAlight, and periphial \AxA\ are all considered small systems.
Here \AxAlight\ referes to light ion collisions where A$^{l}\lesssim 30$.
Light ion data has become available only recently at RHIC () and the LHC

Evidence for QGP comes from partonic energy loss and collective flow (among others).
As the system size decreases from central \AxA\ a weakening of these experimental signatures is expected as the fireball (if any) shrinks smaller and smaller.

Flow like signatrues have been oberved in \pxp~\cite{HION-2015-09}, \pxPb~\cite{HION-2012-13}, \pxAu, \dxAu, \HexAu~\cite{PHENIX:SmallFlow}, \OxO, \NexNe~\cite{HION-2025-02}, and perhiphral \PbxPb~\cite{HION-2016-06}.

Measurments of collectivity have been applied to even ``smaller'' systems than the ones mentioned above such as electron positron annihilation with the ALEPH dectector at LEP~\cite{Belle:collective}.
The collectivity signal in $e^{+}e^{-}$ collisions was not significant and could be explained by \textsc{Pythia}6.

In, \pxp\ and \pxPb\ collisions there is no unambigous evidence for partonic energy loss.

The claim of no jet energy loss in \pxp\ collisions is substantiated by the great sucess of pQCD calculations in describing \pxp\ experimental data.
For example, looking at the balace of a EW vector boson ($\gamma$, $Z$) recoiling against a jet one finds that on average the EW probe and the jet match NLO predictions~\cite{STDM-2011-27}.
Since the EW probe does not interact stronly, any partonic energy loss would only impact the jet.
Therefore, if there was any jet quenching in \pxp\ collisions, there would a net imbalance in the system which would disagree with pQCD predictions.
Such an imbalance is observed in $\gamma +$jet systems in \PbxPb\ collisions~\cite{HION-2022-14}.
