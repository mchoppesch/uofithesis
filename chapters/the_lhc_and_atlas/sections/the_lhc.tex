\subsection{the Large Hadron Collider}

The Large Hadron Collider~\cite{Evans:2008zzb} (LHC) is the worlds largest and highest energy particle accelerator and is a part of the Center for European Nuclear Research (CERN).
Situated on the Franco-Swiss border near Geneva, Switzerland the LHC resides in a 27 km underground tunnel orriginally built for the Large Electron-Positron (LEP) collider in the late 1980s.
By 2000 the LEP was turned off and the tunnel was liberated for the installation of the LHC, and by 2008 the first proton beams were circulating.
The LHC was designed to study the Standard Model of physics with \pxp\ collisions up to a center of mass energy of 14 \TeV\footnote{The LHC has not yet reached its design energy}.
The LHC has four large experiments along its beamline.
ATLAS (A Torodial Lhc ApparatuS)~\cite{PERF-2007-01} and CMS (Compact Muon Solinoid)~\cite{CMS-TDR-08-001} are general purpose high luminosity experiments with high performace tracking and calorimeter coverage across a wide range of rapidities.
LHCb (LHC beauty) and ALICE (A Large Ion Collider Experiment) are designed for lower luminosity operation.
LHCb utilizes forward tracking and calorimetry to study the $b$-quark.
It also has the unique oppetrtunity to turn the LHC into a fixed target experiment by injecting gas around their interaction point (IP) using the SMOG device.
ALICE is a dedicated experiment designed specifically for ion physics, althogh it also collects and studies \pxp\ data.
While its calorimetry is limited when compared to ATLAS and CMS, ALICE has supurior particle identification (PID) technology.

Beams of protons or ions are provided to the LHC through the CERN accelerator complex~\cite{LHC:Design}, shown in detail in Figure~\ref{fig:cern_accelerator_complex}.
Protons start in LINAC\footnote{Short for linear accelerator} 2 and are injected into a booster while ions start in LINAC 3 and are injected into the Low Energy Ion Ring (LEIR).
Protons start their journey as H$^{-}$ ions that are stripped of their two electons by means of plasma stripping at the begging of the accelerator chain.
${}^{208}$Pb atoms are stripped through a cimbination of plasma and foil stripping of 54 electrons by the time they exit the LEIR.
All species eneter the Proton Syncretron (PS) where they are accelerated to an energy of 26 \GeV/Z.
They then are injected into the Super Proton Syncretron (SPS) where they are further accelerated to an energy of 450 \GeV/Z.
Finally the beams can be injected into the LHC, where two countercirculating beams are filled one at at time.
Between the SPS and the LHC, Pb${}^{54+}$ ions are stripped of their remaining electrons resulting in Pb${}^{82+}$.

The LHC has a 40 MHz frequency clock, and each tick represents a equally spaced \text{bunch crossing IDs} (BCIDs) in which a longitudianlly compact bunch of particles can be injected.
Given the length of the LHC, and that the particles are relativistic there are 3,560 BCIDs in total spaced 25 ns apart.
Only a fraction of the BCIDs are occupied in LHC running, where gaps between particles are needed in order to be able to dump the beams.
In \pxp\ collisions the BCIDs are filled in a series, meaning one filled bunch can preceed another filled bunch 25ns later.
To account for the higher occupency of detectors in ion collisions, among other things, Ion beams are filled with empty BCIDs in between filled bunches.
After BCIDs are filled the LHC ramps beams up to a top energy of 7 \TeV/Z\footnote{The top energy so far has been 6.8 \TeV in Run 3.}.
The top energy for ion beams is limited by the Z/A ratio of the colliding species.
For example, in the maximum energy for \PbxPb\ ions with a Z/A of 82/208 would be 2.76 \TeV per nucleon for a center of mass collision energy of 5.52 \TeV, though this has never been achived.
The LHC deliveres luminosity to experiments via two countercirculating beams.
The two beams are made to cross one another at the four IPs corrusponding to the four experiments.

The flagship high-luminosity \pxp\ program at the LHC has operated for 3 ``runs'' or extended periods of data taking.  Run 1 (2009--2013), which collected the data that discovered the Higgs Boson~\cite{HIGG-2012-27,CMS-HIG-13-001}, accumulated $\approx$30 \FB\ of data at \Spp\ of 7 and 8 \TeV.
Runs 2 (2015-2018) and 3 (2022-2026) added 160 \FB\ at \Spp\ = 13 \TeV\ and 355 \FB\ at 13.6 \TeV\ respectivly.

In addition to its flagship high luminosity \pxp\ program the LHC also provides collisions aimed specifically for the field of nuclear physics.
A list of these periods are compiled in Table~\ref{tab:lhc_nuclear_runs}.
These include heavy ion runs (A+A) to study QGP physics, small systems runs (\pxPb\ and $A_{\mathrm{light}}+A_{\mathrm{light}}$) to study cold nuclear matter effects and search for the turn on of QGP physics, and \pxp\ reference runs as a comparision point for the other systems at the same energy.
The author of this dissertation was on site assiting with operations and data taking for all Run 3 ion runs sans the 2026 \PbxPb\ run.

\subsubsection{2016 \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}+Pb Data}
In 2016, the LHC collided the asymmetric proton--Lead (\pxPb) system.
Two different center of mass energies were used, \SNN = 5.02 \TeV\ and \SNN = 8.16 \TeV.
The luminosity sampled by the higher energy sample far exceeded that of the lower energy sample.  Part of the reason for that discrepency comes from the difference in the average number of collisions per bunch crossing (\avmu) in each sample is much higher in the \SNN = 8.16 \TeV\ sample.
Higher \avmu\ also means higher level of contamination from in-time and out-of-time pileup events\footnote{In-time pileup refers to two collisions happening in the same BCID, out-of-time pileup refers to a collion from a previous BCID somehow degrading the measurment in a current BCID}.

Another particular feature of the 2016 \pxPb\ dataset it fact that the direction of circulation for the proton and lead beams switched in the middle of the run.  In the first hi-\avmu\ period, \textbf{Period B}, protons circulated in the accelerator in a clockwise direction\footnote{Direction defined from viewing the LHC from above} while lead ions circulated in the counterclockwise direction.  In the second hi-\avmu\ period, \textbf{Period C}, this directionality was reveresed.

\subsubsection{2025 Light Ion Data}
In summer 2025, the LHC ran a short dedicated light ion data taking period.
This included runs of \pxO, \OxO, and \NexNe.
The \pxO\ data was taken at maximum energy due to a request from cosmic ray community.
The \OxO\ and \NexNe\ data was taken at 5.36 \TeV\ in order to match the Run 3 \PbxPb\ and \pxp\ \SNN, even though the accelerator could have produced slightly higher energy collisions.
The LHC performed excellently and the \OxO\ luminoisty recorded of 8~\NB\ was much higher than even optimistic projections of 2~\NB.
Further to this point the \NexNe\ accumulating 1~\NB\ was unexpected given that there was only one LHC fill allocated.
In order reduce complexity from the LHC side for this short run, only a small number of very widely spaced bunches were injected into the beams.
This means that the \OxO\ and \NexNe\ data have negligable out-of-time pileup effects.
