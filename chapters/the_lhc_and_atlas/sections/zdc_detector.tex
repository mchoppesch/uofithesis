\subsection{The Zero Degree Calorimeter and Technical Work}
\label{sec:ZDC}
\subsubsection{The Zero Degree Calorimeter}
The ATLAS Zero Degree Calorimeter (ZDC) is designed to detect neutral particles that very close to the beampipe~\cite{PERF-2007-01, GENR-2019-02, ZDC:Run3Tech}. It consists of two detectors located in absorbers along the beamline $\pm 140$ m from the ATLAS interaction point. Each side (or arm) consists of four modules each with a material budget of 1.14 hadronic interaction lengths.  The absorber layers consist of tungsten plates, and the active layers consist of Cherenkov radiators.  These Cherenkov radiators transport Cherenkov radiation\footnote{Cherenkov radiation is emitted in a mach cone when particles travel faster than the local speed of light in the material medium} induced by the particle shower to the lens of a Photomultiplier tube (PMT).  Each of the four modules are instrumented with a single PMT.  Cherenkov radiators were chosen since they offer substantial radiation hardness, necessary for a location that receives a very high dose.  In Run 2, the active layers consisted of Quartz rods as the active layer.  The radiation hardness of the detector was improved considerably in Run 3 when the active layer was replaced with doped fused silica rods.
Several views for a 3D model of the ZDC in Run~3 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:zdc_detector_overview}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=\linewidth,
        alt={Three views of the ATLAS Zero Degree Calorimeter: a three-dimensional view, a side view of a module, and a top view of the module active area.}
    ]{chapters/the_lhc_and_atlas/figures/zdc_detector/ZDCInt.001.png}
    \caption{The ATLAS ZDC. Left: 3D view of the calorimeter as set up for the 2016 \pxPb\ run. Center: Side view of a ZDC module. Right: Top view of the module active area. Figure provided by R. Longo.}

As a calorimeter, the ZDC destructively samples the energy of the particles that enter it.
Since dipole magnets between the IP and the ZDC swipe away forward charged particles, only neutral particles enter the ZDC.
In ion running, the nucleons that do not participate in the collisions number $2A - $\NPART\ and are termed ``spectator'' nucleons.
Measurements of the energy flow at zero degrees can provide input into the initial conditions of the QGP.
However, not all spectator nucleons are neutrons.
Charged spectators, like protons or charged nuclear fragments will be bent out of the ZDC acceptance.
Therefore, the particles that do impinge on the ZDC are a subset of the total number of spectators that are electrically neutral.
Since these ``spectator'' neutrons do not interact in the collision at the IP they will impact the ZDC with roughly the same per nucleon energy as the beam.
The number of spectator neutrons in non-trivially dependent on the event geometry, but they range from zero to roughly $(A-Z)/2$.

Additionally, ZDC are useful in the study of UPCs.
The pattern of energy in the ZDC can inform what type of process occurred at the IP, by counting the number of neutrons in each arm of the ZDC, i.e. on each side of the IP.
The $0n0n$ class denotes a coherent photon-photon process ($\gamma \gamma$), $0nXn/Xn0n$ represents a photo-nuclear interaction where the target nucleus breaks up resulting in forward neutron production ($\gamma A / A\gamma$), and $XnXn$ identifies a hadronic interaction with neutral spectators on both sides.
The ZDC can be used to trigger on these processes at the hardware level.
The correlation between energy deposited in either arm of the ZDC is displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:zdc_upc_vs_had} for a UPC selection and a hadronic selection. The UPC selection is dominated by photon-photon and photonuclear events, while the hadronic selection is dominated by the $XnXn$ topology.

    \caption{Correlation between the energy deposited in the two arms of the ZDC for a UPC selection (left) and a hadronic selection (right).}
    \label{fig:zdc_upc_vs_had}

In order to capture signals from a zero to eighty-plus neutrons, the ZDC operates with two sets of readout instrumentation, denoted as high and low gain Analog-to-Digital Converters (ADCs).  The high gain readout is configured to have a dynamic range that saturates with a couple of neutrons.  The low gain readout has a range of a roughly ten times that of the high gain readout.
%Each readout consisted of two 40 MHz ADCs (25 ns).  Both ADCs read out the same signal, but one is delayed by 12.5 ns.  This allows for the combined readout to have a sampling of 80 MHz (12.5 ns).

The ZDC reconstruction software transforms the raw data read out from the above ADCs into module specific amplitudes.
Firstly, each waveform must have its pedestal subtracted in order to make sure that the output amplitude represents only strength of the module signal and not any offset caused by the electronics configuration.  Then, the subtracted samples can be fit to a functional waveform.  The amplitude that minimize the $\chi^2$ of this fit is then considered to be the amplitude in that given event.
%\TCR{Prob need to include more details about the ZDC reconstruction}
Finally, the ZDC calibration transforms the above amplitudes, which have units of ADC counts, into units of energy.

\subsubsection{Technical Work: ZDC L1 Trigger Simulation}
As part of the process of becoming an ATLAS author, a prospective member is asked to complete a technical task as a contribution to the experiment.
The task assigned to the author of this dissertation was to implement software simulating the new ZDC trigger configuration in Run~3 to properly estimate rates for novel trigger combination from Run~2 data.

\paragraph{Motivation for new ZDC trigger in Run~3}
The ZDC trigger in Run~2 was designed from a simple NIM threshold for either ZDC arm, partitioning the trigger combinations into the classes described in the previous section, namely $\gamma \gamma$, $\gamma A$, and hadronic. The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:zdc-trigger} displays this trigger scheme.

The ZDC received upgrades before Run 3~\cite{ZDC:Run3Tech}, the most relevant here being the new front-end and readout ``LUCROD'' electronics.  The LUCROD boards have a sampling rate of 320 MHz and allow for a fully digital trigger.
This digital trigger allows for a more complicated trigger scheme to be implemented in Run 3; the $Xn$ (any neutron) class was further broken up into $1-4n$ (one to four neutrons) and $>5n$ (five or more neutrons). The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:zdc-trigger} displays this new trigger scheme for Run~3.

Discriminating between these two classes is motivated by the prevalence of coincident UPC processes at LHC energies.
The most common UPC process in \PbxPb\ collisions at LHC energies is the electromagnetic dissociation (EMD) of the Pb nucleus, occurring at a rate of around $200$ b for the excitation of a single nucleus~\cite{ALICE:EMD}.
EMD proceeds by the exchange of a soft photon between nuclei, which excites the target nucleus into a resonant mode; this nucleus decays by low-energy emission of a few ($1-4$) neutrons.
Since the neutrons are emitted in the rest frame of the beam energy nucleus, they are typically contained in the acceptance of the ZDC~\cite{ZDC:Run3Tech}.
Hadronic backgrounds in the $1-4n$ neutron class can be removed by a simple total $E_{\mathrm{T}}$ requirement, since hadronic events with a small number of neutrons will be very-central and thus have high event activity.
EMD often occurs coincidentally with other UPC processes under study.
This coincident production causes migrations in the neutron classes defined above.
For example, a coherent photon-photon interaction accompanied by EMD with neutron emission on one (both) side(s) will migrate from the $0n0n$ class to the $0nXn/Xn0n$ ($XnXn$) class.
Similarly, a photo-nuclear interaction can migrate from the $0nXn/Xn0n$ class to the $XnXn$ class.
While these migrations can complicate the interpretation of UPC measurements in an exclusive neutron class, they also offer an opportunity to study the impact parameter dependence of UPC processes.
For example, the impact parameter dependence of dielectron production~\cite{HION-2021-16} and nuclear PDF modification~\cite{HION-2024-12} have been studied by ATLAS.

The ATLAS trigger simulation algorithm for extrapolating experimentally observed rates to different pileup conditions (\avmu) implicitly assumes that the process under study is sufficiently rare. (i.e. that the number of coincident triggers for a certain process is very small)
Since EM dissociation has a very large cross section, this assumption was not valid for the ZDC triggers being considered.
At higher instantaneous luminosity, EM pileup rates increase accordingly and increase the probability for the type of migrations discuss above.
To study this effect, a pileup dependent correction was derived following Reference~\cite{EM:pileupcorr}.
Assuming Poisson statistics apply, the raw triggered rate for any number of a  given process can be calculated as $R^{t} = f  \times \left(1- e^{-\mu_{\mathrm{process}}}\right)$ where $f$ is the frequency of the LHC corrected for the number of bunches,  and $\mu_{\mathrm{process}}$ is the average number of processes per bunch crossing.
Then a trigger rate for a single process is $R = f \times \mu_{\mathrm{process}} $.
The EM pileup corrected rate for a process that puts neutrons in both the A and C arms of the ZDC can be written as,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lumicorr}
    \begin{aligned}
        R_{\mathrm{AC}} &= -f \ln \left(e^{-\mu_{\mathrm{A C}}}\right)
        = -f \ln \left(e^{\mu_{\mathrm{AC \cup A \cup C}} - \mu_{\mathrm{AC \cup A}} - \mu_{\mathrm{AC \cup C}}}\right) \\
        &= -f \ln \left[\frac{\left(1-R^{t}_{\mathrm{AC}}/f - R^{t}_{\mathrm{A}}/f\right)\left(1-R^{t}_{\mathrm{AC}}/f - R^{t}_{\mathrm{C}}/f\right)}{\left(1-R^{t}_{\mathrm{AC}}/f - R^{t}_{\mathrm{A}}/f - R^{t}_{\mathrm{C}}/f\right)}\right]
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Where $R^{t}_{\mathrm{AC}}$ is the number of triggered events for an ``AC'' process, $R^{t}_{\mathrm{A}}$ is the number of triggered events for an ``A'' process, and  $R^{t}_{\mathrm{C}}$ is the number of triggered events for a ``C'' process.
The raw and pileup-corrected AC trigger rates over time are compared in Figure~\ref{fig:zdc-trigger-pileup-correction}.
The instantaneous luminosity falls over time as the density of filled bunches is depleted through collisions and beam burnoff.
The AC ZDC rate is very sensitive to the changing \avmu\ but the correction in Equation~\ref{eq:lumicorr} is able to correctly describe and remove this effect.
By understanding how the rates for ZDC triggers can change with \avmu\ it is possible to extrapolate across different run conditions.
This was used to help predict the rates for ZDC triggers before the start of the first Run~3 \PbxPb\ run.

\subsubsection{Technical Work: 2016 \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}+Pb ZDC Calibration}

Due to the asymmetric nature of the \pxPb\ collisions system in each period only one arm of the ZDC detector will receive ion fragments.
In 2019, J. Nagle performed \TCR{ ref needed} the calibration of the 5.02 \TeV\ data, but concluded that the contamination from pileup in the high-\avmu\ \pxPb\ data made a calibration unfeasible.
The author of this dissertation undertook the calibration of the high high-\avmu\ \pxPb\ data in 2023 after substantial improvements had been made to the ZDC reconstruction in order to mitigate the previously untenable issues.
This technical work enabled the measurement detailed in~\ref{ch:pPb}.

\paragraph{Improvements to ZDC Reconstruction}
The first improvement made to the ZDC reconstruction comes from~\cite{ATL-COM-PHYS-2019-1454}.  This note documents updates to the signal processing created mainly for the 2018 \PbxPb\ data, but that are useful for the 2016 \pxPb\ data as well.  These updates includes significant improvements to the reconstructions ability to fit pulses in the presence of out of time (OOT) pile up, both preceding and after the event in question.  Large OOT pileup pulses would saturate the high gain ADC, leading to the pulse being analyzed with the low gain ADC which has a roughly ten times worse resolution.  By excluding a few samples from the beginning or (end) of these pulses and looking at the high gain ADC values, the reconstruction will yield a reasonable fit for the current event. This is especially important given that the relatively high \avmu\ in the 2016 8.16 \TeV\ \pxPb\ has more events affected by out of time pileup.

The second significant improvement made to the ZDC reconstruction was the `two-pass' analysis method.  This method first uses a relatively high second derivative threshold to search for pulses in a ZDC event.  This high threshold helps to suppress noise from small background and pileup pulses.  However, if at least one module has a pulse that passes this threshold, all the modules on said ZDC arm are reanalyzed with a lower second derivative threshold. This `repass' recovers finer pulses that were too small to be `seen' on the first pass.

The third significant improvement\footnote{This improvement was done by the author of the dissertation, while the first two were done by Pengqi Yin of Columbia University}, involves the studies on the pulse timing.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.55\linewidth]{chapters/the_lhc_and_atlas/figures/zdc_detector/T0plot1.pdf}
    \caption{Example of a fitted ZDC pulse, taken from \cite{ATL-COM-PHYS-2019-1454} }
    \label{fig:pulse}
\end{figure}

Base ZDC pulses (see Figure~\ref{fig:pulse}) are fit to a function of the form given in Equation~\ref{eqn:fermiexp}, hereafter referred to as a FermiExp pulse.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:fermiexp}
    f(t\mid A,\tau_1,\tau_2,t_0,C) = A \left( \frac{e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_2}}{1+e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_1}} \right)+C
\end{equation}

Several of these parameters are fixed in order for the fitting function to have fewer degrees of freedom and thus converge more efficiently.  For example, $\tau_1\ \&\ \tau_2$ are fixed in the 2016 ZDC reconstruction.

From a data quality standpoint, it would be useful to be able to reject pulses that were relatively early or late when compared to other pulses. One way to do this would be by defining a nominal $t_0$ range that is acceptable.  However, there are two main challenges to defining a nominal $t_0$.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item When the high voltage powering the a ZDC PMT changes, it affects the transit time in the tube which leads to a slightly different nominal $t_0$. \newline \textbf{Solution:} Implement a time dependent nominal $t_0$ correction for the entire dataset.
    \item There is a nonlinear dependence of the $t_0$ on the pulse amplitude that must be corrected.  \newline \textbf{Solution:} Fit the curvature induced by the nonlinear dependence to a 5th order polynomial.  Use that fit function to flatten the convex distribution.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Data selection}

The data used for the calibration of the high-\avmu\ \pxPb\ was read out into a dedicated ZDC calibration stream.  This dedicated readout only stores information about the ZDC detector for each accepted event\footnote{The per-event data footprint of the ZDC detector is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the whole ATLAS detector including all detector subsystems} and can therefore accumulate substantial statistics needed for a precision calibration.  Events entering the stream fired the \triga~or~\trigc\ triggers, requiring that there was some substantial energy in at least one arm of the ZDC.  To get a set of events unbiased by trigger turn on thresholds, the set of events used to analyze Side A were triggered on a signal in Side C and vice versa.

\paragraph{Calibration procedure}
The ZDC is designed to sample neutral ion fragments with the same per-nucleon energy as the beam.  The lightest neutral fragment possible is a single beam energy neutron incident on the detector.  A single neutron that fragmented from a lead ion will, on average, be as energetic as the per-nucleon beam energy, which here is 2.5625~\TeV.  Therefore, the energy of single neutrons gives a well defined energy to calibrate against.  Of course single neutrons will not have the exact per-nucleon beam energy every time, since the exact energy of bound nucleons is stochastically modified by the so-called `fermi-momentum'~\TCR{citation needed}.  The variance of single neutrons given by fermi-momentum coupled with the experimental resolution of the ZDC should give the width of the detector response to a single neutron.

Therefore the single neutron peak~\footnote{observed detector response to single neutrons} intrinsically contains information about the ZDC Energy Scale ($\mu_{\mathrm{1n}}$) and ZDC Energy Resolution ($\sigma_{\mathrm{1n}}$).  However, we are interested in not just any calibration, but the \textit{optimal} one. The optimal calibration is defined to be the one that puts the mean of the single neutron peak at 2.5625~\TeV\ and minimizes the variance (therefore maximizing experimental resolution).  The machinery of the calibration works as follows.

The goal is to find a set of gains $\mathbf{g} = \{g_0,g_1,g_2,g_3\}$ such that given the ZDC module amplitudes for a given event $\mathbf{A} = \{A_0,A_1,A_2,A_3\}$ the dot product $\mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A}$ is equal to the energy of the ZDC in units of \GeV (2562.5 \GeV).  The below calibration procedure is performed on a subset of ZDC events in the first neutron peak.  Equations~\ref{1nmean}~and~\ref{1nvar} are the mean an variance of the first neutron peak sample. The variable $N$ indicates the size of the sample used to perform the analysis.
\begin{equation}
    \label{1nmean}
    E_{\text{1n}} = \bigl \langle \mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A} \bigr \rangle_{N}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \label{1nvar}
    \sigma^2 = \Bigl \langle \left( \mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A} \right)^2  \Bigr \rangle_{N} - \left( \bigl \langle  \mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A}  \bigr \rangle_{N} \right)^2 = \Bigl \langle \left( \mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A} \right)^2  \Bigr \rangle_{N} - \left( \text{E}_{\text{1n}} \right)^2
\end{equation}
Using the method of Lagrange multipliers, one can minimize the variance under the constraint that mean of the one neutron peak is equal to the per nucleon beam energy.
\begin{equation}
    \label{LagrangeMult}
    \mathcal{L} = \Bigl \langle \left( \mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A} \right)^2  \Bigr \rangle_{\text{N}} - \left( \text{E}_{\text{1n}} \right)^2 + \lambda\left( \bigl \langle \mathbf{g}^T\mathbf{A} \bigr \rangle_{\text{N}} -  \text{E}_{\text{1n}}\right)
\end{equation}
Solving \ref{LagrangeMult} with respect to $\mathbf{g}$ and $\lambda$ yields the following system of equations.
\begin{equation}
    \label{LagrangeMatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \; 2 \,\langle A_0 A_0 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_0 A_1 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_0 A_2 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_0 A_3 \rangle & \langle A_0 \rangle \\
        \; 2 \,\langle A_1 A_0 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_1 A_1 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_1 A_2 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_1 A_3 \rangle & \langle A_1 \rangle \\
        \; 2 \,\langle A_2 A_0 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_2 A_1 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_2 A_2 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_2 A_3 \rangle & \langle A_2 \rangle \\
        \; 2 \,\langle A_3 A_0 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_3 A_1 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_3 A_2 \rangle & 2 \,\langle A_3 A_3 \rangle & \langle A_3 \rangle \\
        \;  \langle A_0 \rangle & \langle A_1 \rangle & \langle A_2 \rangle & \langle A_3 \rangle & 0 \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \; g_0 \\
        \; g_1 \\
        \; g_2 \\
        \; g_3 \\
        \; \lambda \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \; 0 \\
        \; 0 \\
        \; 0 \\
        \; 0 \\
        \; E_{1n} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

The ZDC energy response changes significantly over the period of a run.  Therefore, it is imperative to create a time dependent calibration.

The software to implement this calibration was created by the author of this dissertation.  Some important practical details are described below.  Firstly, the data is broken a series of separate consecutive time intervals with the condition that each time interval has about a million events, which was found to be sufficient for a stable calibration.  Next, within each interval, a  histogram is created out of the sum of ZDC module amplitudes for a given event $\Sigma_i^{4} A_i$.  The single neutron peak is apparent in the spectrum of $\Sigma_i^{4} A_i$, and a window $\pm 1.5\sigma$ around the peak is defined by a simple Gaussian fit.
Then, for each time interval, fourteen histograms are defined corresponding to the unaveraged matrix elements of Equation~\ref{LagrangeMatrix} and are filled with events falling inside the single neutron window defined in the previous step.
Next the histograms are averaged over and Equation~\ref{LagrangeMatrix} can be solved for the optimal \textbf{g} for each time interval.  Once the optimal \textbf{g} for every time interval is decided, a cubic spline is used to interpolate smoothly between the successive time intervals.  Then, on an event by event basis the calibrated ZDC energy is equal to $\mathbf{g(t)}^T\mathbf{A} $.
