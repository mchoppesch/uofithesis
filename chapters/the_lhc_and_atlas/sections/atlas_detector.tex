\subsection{ATLAS detector}

The ATLAS detector~\cite{PERF-2007-01, GENR-2019-02} is a general-purpose particle physics detector with symmetrical cylindrical geometry and nearly 4$\pi$ solid angle coverage.\footnote{\AtlasCoordFootnote}
The detector components include inner tracking detectors encased in a superconducting solenoid magnet, electromagnetic (EM) and hadronic calorimeters, and a muon spectrometer which includes three large superconducting toroid magnets.
The author of this dissertation is a member of the ATLAS collaboration, and the ATLAS detector and software suite were used for all measurements described.

\subsubsection{Inner Detector}
The inner detector (ID) system tracks charged particles within the range $|\eta|<2.5$ and full coverage in azimuth.
The ID lies inside of a 2 T axial magnetic field, which bends the charged particles so their momentum can be measured.
Counting outward from the beampipe, the first component is the Insertable B-Layer (IBL), which was a new silicon tracker for Run 2 and enhanced tracking performance in high-luminosity data-taking.
The IBL is required to be radiation hard given its close proximity to the beam pipe.
The Pixel detector is next, which is another silicon tracker.
The IBL and Pixel typically provide 4 tracking points for charged particles.
The SemiConductor Tracker (SCT) is a larger, more distant tracker that typically provides 8 tracking points.
Finally, the last layer of the ID is the Transition Radiation Detector (TRT), which uses straw drift tubes filled with a Xe-based gas mixture.
The TRT provides basic PID as it is able to discriminate between electrons and photons.
Altogether, the ID provided precise tracking of charged particles with \PT\ > 0.5 \GeV.

\subsubsection{Calorimeter System}
The ATLAS calorimeter system consists of two parts, a Liquid Argon (LAr) calorimeter and a scintillating tile calorimeter.
The system destructively samples particles over a wide range of pseudorapidity (|$\eta$|<4.9) with full coverage in azimuth.
The sampling calorimeter design consists of interspersed absorber material, which cause the incident particles to shower, and active material, which output a signal proportional to the energy deposited by a secondary particle shower crossing that layer.
Calorimeters typically consist of an EM and then a hadronic calorimeter, where the EM calorimeters are stationed before the hadronic calorimeters.
This configuration is used because electrons and photons will lose energy over relatively short distances due to pair production and bremsstrahlung while hadrons take longer distances to shower.
The LAr calorimeter consists of an EM calorimeter, a hadronic end-cap calorimeter (HEC), and a Forward Calorimeter (FCal).
All the parts of the LAr calorimeter use liquid argon as the active material.
The EM calorimeter uses Pb plates as passive material.
Copper was used for the HEC and copper+tungsten were used for the FCal.
Since the HEC and FCal are more geometrically constrained, copper helps with heat dissipation.
The tile calorimeter sits outside of the LAr calorimeter in the central $\eta$ region; it uses steel tiles as an absorber and scintillating tiles as the active material.
A diagram of the calorimeter system, with the different subcomponents highlighted, can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:atlas_calorimeter_cross_section}.
Different $\eta$ coverages are denoted on the plot.

In ATLAS, transverse energy (\ET\ ) deposited in the FCal is the most common type of centrality estimator (CE) used for Heavy Ion analyses.
The \ET\ can be shown to be very well correlated to the charged particle multiplicity at midrapidity.
This \ET\ is calibrated to the EM scale, so the absolute energy scale is not guaranteed to be correct when hadrons impinge on the detector.
One problem that can affect centrality determination in this way is the presence of out-of-time pileup.
The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:atlas_calorimeter_material_pulse} shows an example of a pulse shape in the LAr calorimeter.
One can see that after a large pulse, the LAr readout amplitude will go negative for a long time (around 25 BCIDs).
For heavy ion collisions, this can mean that an event that is preceded by another high-activity event can have its energy measurement biased.
This is one of the reasons out-of-time pileup must be rejected in analyses utilizing centrality.

\subsubsection{Muon Spectrometer}
Muons weakly interact with ordinary matter and as such are able to escape the ID and calorimeter systems while losing little energy.
As such, a dedicated spectrometer is built surrounding these systems to identify and track muons over a pseudorapidity range of $|\eta| < 2.7$  with full coverage in azimuth.
The muons are deflected by a set of superconducting air-core toroidal magnets with a strength of 2.0--6.0 T$\cdot$m.
The spectrometer is built out of three layers, each made up of monitored drift tubes.
To provide additional tracking precision, cathode-strip chambers were implemented in the endcap region in Run~2, but these were replaced by the so-called ``New Small Wheel'' detectors, which are small-strip thin-gap chambers and Micromegas chambers in Run~3.
The muon trigger covers a slightly smaller range of pseudorapidity of $|\eta| < 2.4$.

\subsubsection{Forward Detectors}
The ATLAS forward detectors lie along the beampipe and cover very forward ranges of rapidity detecting particles that are missed by the coverage of the ATLAS central detector.
Figure~\ref{fig:atlas_forward_detector_placement} shows the location of all the Forward detectors with respect to the beampipe and central detector.
Note that at $\pm$140 m the two counter-circulating beams are split by magnets and go into separate beampipes for transport around the LHC.  Close to the IP the beams are kept in a single pipe.

\paragraph{LUCID}
As described in Section~\ref{sec:lumi}, luminosity is crucial for calibrating experimental observations to absolute cross sections.
LUCID (Luminosity measurement using Cherenkov Integrating Detector) is a forward detector, located at $\pm$17 m from the IP, primarily dedicated to Luminosity measurement.
It detects inelastic scatterings in the forward direction using Cherenkov light produced in quartz windows located close to the beampipe.
LUCID can provide both online and offline luminosity information.

\paragraph{ALFA}
ALFA (Absolute Luminosity For ATLAS) measures the absolute luminosity by measuring elastic scatterings at very small angles.
It is located $\pm$240 m from the ATLAS IP.
They are measured by so-called ``Roman pots'' that can be moved very close to the beam itself, requiring specialized beam conditions.

\paragraph{AFP}
AFP (Atlas Forward Proton) also utilizes Roman Pot style detectors, located at $\pm$205 m and $\pm$217 m of the IP.
It is also used for studying elastic \pxp\ collisions.

\paragraph{ZDC} The Zero Degree Calorimeter (ZDC) is a detector employed mostly for heavy ion specific runs in the LHC.
Due to the high radiation environment in high-\avmu\ \pxp\ runs, the ZDC is removed.
The author of this dissertation is a member of the ZDC group and as such this detector deserves a more detailed discussion, which is given in Section~\ref{sec:ZDC}.

\subsubsection{Trigger and Software}
\label{sec:ATLASTrig}
ATLAS uses a sophisticated trigger system to select events of interest for further analysis in order to cope with the large 40 MHz rate of bunch crossings~\cite{TRIG-2016-01,TRIG-2022-01}.
The first-level trigger, usually called L1 or hardware trigger, is implemented with custom triggering hardware to identify events.
This reduces the sample rate of events to around 100 kHz.
The selection at L1 is course and typically needs to be refined with a high-level trigger (HLT) where algorithms are run on a limited set of online reconstructed physics objects providing more sensitivity to physics processes of interest.
This HLT selection further reduces the event rate at about 1.25 kHz and 3 kHz in Run~2 and Run~3, respectively.
Events are then written to a disk and saved for offline analysis.

Triggers in ATLAS are named according to the concatenation of their HLT tag and L1 tag.
For example, consider a minimum bias trigger with the HLT tag \texttt{HLT\_mb\_sptrk} and the L1 tag \texttt{L1\_RDO\_FILLED}.
The L1 tag describes a random first-level trigger with a requirement that requires the interacting BCIDs to be filled with beam particles.
The HLT tag requires that the software trigger check for the presence of at least one charged particle track in the ID.
The total name of this trigger is \texttt{HLT\_mb\_sptrk\_L1\_RDO\_FILLED}; it is called a minimum bias trigger because it makes very little requirements on the event of interest.

Other triggers are more specialized and are designed to select events with various specific physics signatures present.
Jet triggers are designed to select a sub-sample of all collisions that contain jets of a certain \PT.
For example, the \texttt{HLT\_j60\_L1\_J20} chooses events where the hardware trigger evaluates the event to contain at least one jet with \PT\ > 20 \GeV\ and the high-level trigger evaluates the event to contain at least one jet with \PT\ > 60 \GeV.
The L1 jet selection is typically courser (i.e. less accurate), which is why the \PT\ threshold is lower than the HLT selection.
In a similar way, jet triggers can also select events in certain ranges of $\eta$ to create an sample of only forward jets.

To not saturate available bandwidth for writing events, triggers that select events at a high rate are typically prescaled.
This means that only a fraction of total events that fired a given trigger are kept for analysis.
The triggers \textit{prescale} is a number that describes the ratio of triggered events relative to events written to disk.
A trigger with a prescale of ten will fire ten times for every one event kept and it will sample 10\% of the total luminosity seen by the experiment.
Triggers selecting relatively rare processes are typically unprescaled, meaning they have a prescale of one and every event is kept.
For example, because low-$Q^2$ QCD processes are far more likely to happen than high-$Q^2$ processes low-\PT\ jet triggers are typically prescaled while high-\PT\ jet triggers are typically unprescaled.
Precales can be applied to both L1 and HLT items, and the total prescale is the product of the two.

A trigger designed to select a certain physics object, like a jet, may have a characteristic inefficiency.
Trigger efficiencies can be evaluated using fully efficient monitoring triggers which is independent of the signal trigger while providing good statistical precision.
Typically, minimum bias triggers are used as monitoring triggers.
The efficiency can be written as,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:trigeff}
    \epsilon_{\mathrm{Trigger}} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{Signal}}\cap N_{\mathrm{Monitor}}}{N_{\mathrm{Monitor}}}
\end{equation}
Jet trigger efficiencies are evaluated as a function of \PT\ and are monotonically increasing (sans statistical fluctuations).
The quantity $\PTE^{99}$ represents the \PT\ above which a given jet trigger is more than 99\% efficient at selecting jets.
In both of the analyses presented in this dissertation triggers are always used only above their $\PTE^{99}$ threshold.
For jet triggers that make selections on $\eta$ a similar efficiency must be derived.

Dedicated software~\cite{SOFT-2022-02} is constructed and used by ATLAS in data simulation, in the reconstruction
and analysis of real and simulated data in detector operations, and in the trigger and data acquisition
systems of the experiment.
